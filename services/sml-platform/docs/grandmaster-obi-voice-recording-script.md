# Grandmaster-Obi Academy Voice Recording Script

## Recording instructions — do not read this section aloud

- Record in a quiet room with the same microphone position throughout.
- Use WAV when possible: mono, 48 kilohertz, 24-bit. A high-quality MP3 is acceptable if WAV is unavailable.
- Keep the microphone about six to eight inches from your mouth and slightly off-axis.
- Speak naturally at roughly 105 to 115 words per minute. Do not perform a radio voice.
- Keep mistakes in a separate take. If you stumble, pause for two seconds and restart the full sentence.
- Leave two seconds of silence before the first sentence and five seconds after the last sentence.
- Read the bracketed direction silently, pause, and then deliver the following paragraph in the requested style.
- This script is designed to take roughly thirty minutes when read deliberately with the marked pauses.
- Only submit a recording of your own voice, or a voice you have explicit permission to use.

## Script begins

[Calm, welcoming]

Welcome to the Making Easy Money Academy. I’m Grandmaster-Obi, and I’ll be your guide as we study markets, trading, risk, discipline, and decision-making. The goal is not to memorize a collection of flashy phrases. The goal is to learn how to think clearly when prices are moving, opinions are loud, and time feels short. Good trading begins with patient observation. Great trading adds preparation, risk control, and the humility to admit when the evidence has changed.

Before we begin, take a slow breath. Look at the chart without trying to predict the next candle. Notice the current price, the spread, the volume, the trend, the nearby support and resistance levels, and the time of day. Ask a simple question: what do I know, what do I think, and what would prove me wrong? That question will appear throughout the Academy because disciplined uncertainty is stronger than false confidence.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Conversational]

Markets have a funny way of testing the exact weakness you forgot to include in your plan. If you chase, the price may pull back. If you hesitate, it may run without you. If you double your position because you feel certain, the market may suddenly introduce you to the meaning of the word volatility. That does not mean the market is personally attacking you. It means risk was present before you noticed it.

A professional process gives you something useful to do in each scenario. Before entry, define the setup. At entry, check liquidity and position size. After entry, follow the invalidation rule. After exit, review the decision without rewriting history. A winning trade can contain a bad decision, and a losing trade can contain a good decision. We judge the process first and the outcome second.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Clear teaching voice]

Let’s practice market language. The bid is the highest displayed price a buyer is currently willing to pay. The ask is the lowest displayed price a seller is currently willing to accept. The difference between them is the spread. A narrow spread may indicate active liquidity. A wide spread may indicate uncertainty, low participation, or greater execution risk. The last traded price tells us where the most recent transaction occurred, but it does not guarantee where the next transaction will occur.

Market orders prioritize execution, not price. Limit orders prioritize price, not execution. Stop orders can become market orders when triggered, and they cannot guarantee a fill during a fast move, gap, or trading halt. Every order type solves one problem while leaving another problem open. The correct choice depends on the instrument, the setup, the liquidity, and the consequence of being wrong.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Precise, numerical]

Now I will read prices, percentages, dates, and times. One dollar and five cents. Eighty-three cents. Forty-nine cents. Two dollars and forty-four cents. Three dollars and ten cents. One hundred fifty-seven dollars and twenty-five cents. Seven hundred sixty-two dollars and ninety-eight cents. A gain of two point three four percent. A decline of one point one two percent. A move from forty-three cents to fifty-five cents equals a gain of approximately twenty-seven point nine one percent.

The alert arrived at nine thirty-seven A.M. Eastern Time on September eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. The next observation occurred at nine forty A.M. The three-minute change rate equals the current price minus the price three minutes earlier, divided by the earlier price, multiplied by one hundred. If the current price is one dollar and two cents and the earlier price is one dollar, the change rate is positive two percent. If the current price is ninety-eight cents and the earlier price is one dollar, the change rate is negative two percent.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Deliberate pronunciation]

S.I.R.E. means the Academy’s three-minute price-change measurement. It uses P-now, the current verified price, and P-three-m, the verified price at the exact three-minute baseline. The calculation is: P-now minus P-three-m, divided by P-three-m, multiplied by one hundred. The symbols are sorted internally by that signed percentage. The display shows the ticker, the company name, and the three-minute percentage. It does not present a rank number as if rank were the metric.

When the required baseline is unavailable, the correct output is unavailable, not a guess. New symbols need enough history. A restarted service needs time to rebuild its rolling window. Stale or malformed prices must be rejected. Accuracy is more important than filling every empty cell. A clean dash is better than a confident lie.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Natural ticker reading]

Here are several ticker symbols spoken clearly. S-P-Y. Q-Q-Q. I-W-M. N-V-D-A. T-S-L-A. A-M-D. A-A-P-L. M-S-F-T. A-M-Z-N. M-E-T-A. G-O-O-G-L. C-O-I-N. M-A-R-A. R-I-O-T. G-M-E. A-M-C. R-G-C. R-E-F-R. Z-T-G. A-E-M-D. I-M-C-C. N-C-P-L. P-D-S-B. F-T-F-T. S-N-Y-R. O-L-O-X. R-E-T-O. D-L-X-Y. M-E-D-S. T-C-R-T. Q-C-L-S.

When speaking a ticker in a lesson, pause slightly before the symbol and slightly after it. For example: the scanner detected unusual momentum in N-V-D-A, while the broader index remained nearly flat. Another example: R-G-C moved sharply, but a sharp move by itself does not establish the cause, the durability, or the probability of continuation.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Analytical]

A candlestick records four prices for a selected period: open, high, low, and close. A green candle generally closes above its open. A red candle generally closes below its open. The wick shows the range beyond the candle body. Candle color is descriptive, not predictive. One candle must be interpreted within trend, location, liquidity, volume, volatility, and the behavior of nearby participants.

Support is an area where demand previously became strong enough to slow or reverse a decline. Resistance is an area where supply previously became strong enough to slow or reverse an advance. These are zones, not magical lines. A level can fail. A failed level can become information. A breakout without participation may be fragile. A breakout with expanding volume, improving breadth, and sustained acceptance may deserve more attention, but it still requires an invalidation point.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Energetic but controlled]

Momentum is exciting. Excitement is not a strategy. When the tape speeds up, buyers lift the ask, spreads remain manageable, and price continues to make progress, momentum may be strengthening. When aggressive buying produces little upward progress, a large seller may be absorbing demand. When aggressive selling fails to push price lower, a buyer may be absorbing supply. The tape shows transactions; interpretation still depends on context.

Watch the relationship between effort and result. Heavy volume with limited movement can signal balance or absorption. Light volume with a quick jump can signal thin liquidity rather than strong conviction. Repeated prints at the ask are not automatically bullish if the price cannot advance. Repeated prints at the bid are not automatically bearish if the price refuses to decline. Read what happened, then ask why the expected response did or did not appear.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Serious risk voice]

Never risk money required for rent, food, taxes, healthcare, debt payments, or emergencies. Trading involves substantial risk, including the possible loss of the entire amount committed. Small-cap securities, options, leveraged products, short positions, and extended-hours trading can increase that risk. Liquidity can disappear. Spreads can widen. Trading can halt. Borrow can be recalled. A stop can fill far away from its trigger.

Educational material is not personalized financial advice. An example is not a recommendation. A historical outcome is not a promise. A simulated fill is not the same as an executable live fill. Before taking a position, a learner should understand the instrument, the maximum plausible loss, the planned exit, and the conditions that invalidate the thesis. If those items are unclear, the correct decision may be no trade.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Warm mentor voice]

Discipline is not the absence of emotion. Discipline is the ability to notice emotion without handing it the order ticket. You may feel fear before a valid entry. You may feel excitement during an extended move. You may feel anger after slippage. You may feel the urge to win back a loss immediately. Feelings are real information about your internal state, but they are not market signals.

A useful reset can be simple. Step away from the mouse. Take five slow breaths. Read the original plan. Confirm the position size. Identify the invalidation point. Ask whether new evidence supports the trade or whether pride is keeping it alive. If you cannot answer calmly, reduce risk or stop. Capital preserved today remains available for a better opportunity tomorrow.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Questioning, interactive]

What is the setup? What is the catalyst? Where is liquidity concentrated? What is the average spread? What is the expected holding period? Where is the thesis invalid? How much can be lost if the exit slips? Is the position correlated with another position? Is the market open, premarket, after hours, or closed? Is the displayed price current? Is the data source verified? What evidence would change the plan?

Now consider the opposite side. Who might be selling when you are buying? Who might be buying when you are selling? Are they reducing risk, hedging exposure, providing liquidity, reacting to news, or trading a different time horizon? A market exists because participants disagree about value, timing, or necessity. Understanding that disagreement can improve analysis even when the final decision is to wait.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Options instructor]

An option is a contract connected to an underlying asset. A call gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy under specified terms. A put gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to sell under specified terms. The option buyer pays premium. The option seller receives premium and accepts contractual obligations. Every strategy combines exposure to price, time, volatility, rates, dividends, liquidity, and assignment risk.

Delta estimates how an option’s value may respond to a small move in the underlying, all else equal. Gamma describes how delta may change. Theta describes time decay. Vega describes sensitivity to implied volatility. Rho describes sensitivity to interest rates. These measures are model-based approximations, not guarantees. They can change quickly, particularly near expiration, near the strike, or during large volatility shifts.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Precise options examples]

A covered call combines long shares with a short call. A cash-secured put combines a short put with enough reserved cash to accept assignment. A protective put combines long shares with a long put. A vertical spread buys one option and sells another option of the same type with a different strike. A calendar spread uses different expirations. An iron condor combines defined-risk call and put spreads.

Premium income is not free income. A covered call can limit upside. A cash-secured put can be assigned while the stock continues to fall. Short options can experience early assignment. Wide spreads can make theoretical profit difficult to realize. Implied volatility can decline after an event. Before entering, calculate maximum gain, maximum loss, break-even levels, assignment consequences, and the effect of time.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Storytelling]

Imagine a trader named Jordan arriving before the opening bell. Jordan has three charts open, six alerts firing, a cup of coffee cooling beside the keyboard, and absolutely no written plan. A ticker jumps twelve percent. Jordan buys because the move looks strong. The price immediately drops. Jordan adds because the new price looks cheaper. The spread widens. Trading halts. When trading resumes, the price gaps lower.

Now replay the morning with a process. Jordan identifies the catalyst, checks the float and average volume, marks support and resistance, defines maximum loss, waits for the spread to stabilize, and writes a no-trade condition. The ticker still jumps, but the entry condition never appears. Jordan does nothing. That may feel boring, yet avoiding an undefined risk is a successful decision. The best trade of the morning can be the trade that was rejected for the right reason.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Light humor]

Charts do not know that you promised your group a winner. Candles do not read motivational quotes. The bid does not care how many hours you spent researching, and the ask has never apologized for bad timing. The market is not required to reward effort on your schedule. That is inconvenient, occasionally hilarious, and extremely important.

If your thesis needs the phrase, “it has to come back,” the thesis may need more work. If your risk plan is, “I’ll know when I see it,” you may discover that panic has terrible eyesight. If every losing trade becomes a long-term investment five seconds before the stop, your strategy has quietly changed without permission. Humor helps us remember the lesson, but the lesson remains serious: define decisions before pressure arrives.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Newsroom voice]

At eight thirty A.M. Eastern Time, an economic report was released. Treasury yields moved higher, index futures declined, and volatility increased. The first headline described the number. The second headline offered an interpretation. The third headline repeated a social-media reaction. A careful analyst separates the primary release from commentary about the release.

Check the source, publication time, revision history, and exact language. Distinguish confirmed facts from inference. When reporting an alert, preserve the original timestamp, entry price, target, ticker, and image. When calculating performance, use verified market data and disclose the measurement rule. Peak gain is different from realized gain. A target being touched is different from every member receiving the same fill.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Slow and careful]

Correlation does not prove causation. A pattern discovered after searching thousands of variables may be accidental. Backtests can suffer from survivorship bias, look-ahead bias, selection bias, overfitting, and unrealistic execution assumptions. Transaction costs, spread, slippage, borrow fees, taxes, latency, and capacity can turn an attractive historical result into an unusable live strategy.

Separate training data from testing data. Use walk-forward evaluation when appropriate. Record every transformation. Preserve the original dataset. Define the hypothesis before reviewing the outcome. Compare the strategy with a relevant benchmark. Report drawdown, volatility, hit rate, average win, average loss, payoff ratio, turnover, and exposure. A single impressive return number is not a complete evaluation.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Numbers and ranges]

The portfolio began with ten thousand dollars. The planned risk was one-half of one percent, or fifty dollars. The entry was twenty-four dollars and eighty cents. The invalidation level was twenty-four dollars and thirty cents. The estimated risk per share was fifty cents before slippage. Dividing fifty dollars by fifty cents produces a theoretical size of one hundred shares. If the spread or volatility expands, the trader may reduce that size.

Consider three outcomes. Scenario one gains one hundred dollars. Scenario two loses fifty dollars. Scenario three exits flat before fees. The average result depends on the probability of each scenario, but probabilities are estimates. A positive expected value does not eliminate losing streaks. Position size must remain survivable across a realistic sequence of losses.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Acronyms, then natural explanation]

S-E-C means Securities and Exchange Commission. F-I-N-R-A means Financial Industry Regulatory Authority. E-T-F means exchange-traded fund. I-P-O means initial public offering. E-P-S means earnings per share. P slash E means price-to-earnings ratio. G-D-P means gross domestic product. C-P-I means consumer price index. F-O-M-C means Federal Open Market Committee. O-H-L-C means open, high, low, and close.

R-S-I means relative strength index. M-A-C-D means moving average convergence divergence. V-W-A-P means volume-weighted average price. A-T-R means average true range. O-C-O means one cancels the other. G-T-C means good till canceled. D-T-E means days to expiration. I-V means implied volatility. O-I means open interest. These abbreviations save time, but a learner should understand the underlying idea rather than hiding behind initials.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Firm coaching]

Do not move an invalidation level merely because the loss feels uncomfortable. Do not increase size to repair confidence. Do not confuse a louder opinion with stronger evidence. Do not post a result without its timestamp and measurement method. Do not call a peak move a realized return. Do not present an educational simulation as a guaranteed opportunity.

Instead, document the reason for entry. Document the reason for exit. Save the chart. Record the spread and liquidity. Note whether the market was regular hours or extended hours. Compare the planned outcome with the actual execution. Review recurring errors weekly. Improve one behavior at a time. Consistency is built from small decisions repeated under pressure.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Encouraging]

Progress is not measured by how often you predict the next candle. Progress appears when your process becomes clearer. You wait more patiently. You size more carefully. You recognize when data is stale. You separate evidence from excitement. You can explain a trade before entering and review it honestly after exiting. You can say, “I was wrong,” without turning that sentence into a judgment about your worth.

The Academy is designed for repeated practice. Read the lesson. Use the chart. Test the idea in the simulation. Explain the decision in your own words. Review the answer. Return later and try again under a different market condition. Knowledge becomes skill through deliberate repetition, not through one perfect quiz score.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Different emotional deliveries; pause between sentences]

[Confident] The evidence supports the setup, the risk is defined, and the position size is acceptable.

[Concerned] The spread is widening, volume is thinning, and the original assumption may no longer be valid.

[Curious] What changed during the last three minutes, and which participants may be responsible?

[Excited but controlled] Momentum is accelerating, yet we will wait for the planned trigger.

[Reassuring] Missing one trade does not mean missing every opportunity.

[Direct] Protect capital first.

[Reflective] The outcome surprised me, so I will review the evidence without inventing an excuse.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Pronunciation and articulation]

Verified volatility varies by venue, volume, and valuation. Buyers bid briskly while sellers reassess rapidly. Thin liquidity can create quick, jagged, exaggerated movement. Structured strategies require specific strikes, expirations, premiums, probabilities, and protective rules. Responsible research rejects rumors, records revisions, and recognizes uncertainty.

Three free throws. Six sleek screens. Twelve strong statistics. Twenty-three thoughtful theories. Thirty-three thousand shares. One hundred one lessons across twenty-eight modules. Zero point zero five. One point two five. Ten point seven five percent. Negative three point zero four percent. Nine forty-five and thirty seconds A.M. Eastern Time.

[Pause for three seconds]

[Final lesson voice]

When reviewing a market alert, begin with the original message. Confirm the ticker, entry, target, date, time, and author. Locate verified intraday data. Define the trading session and timezone. Calculate the move using a reproducible formula. Distinguish the target from the peak. State how long the move took. Preserve the evidence. If information is missing, say so plainly.

When reviewing a three-minute signal, record the current price and the price exactly three minutes earlier. Subtract the earlier price from the current price. Divide by the earlier price. Multiply by one hundred. Keep the sign. Positive means price increased. Negative means price decreased. Sort internally when comparison is useful, but display the percentage rather than pretending the position number is the measurement.

[Closing, warm and authoritative]

You have reached the end of this voice recording. Thank you for taking the time to read carefully and naturally. The Making Easy Money Academy exists to help learners become more thoughtful, disciplined, and evidence-driven. Markets will always contain uncertainty. Our job is not to eliminate uncertainty. Our job is to make decisions that respect it.

I’m Grandmaster-Obi. Protect your capital, verify your data, follow your process, and keep learning.
